# 🌍 Episode Research: Carbon Capture
## "The Ghosts, the Pioneers, and the Public Domain"

---

## Episode Premise

Carbon capture research on GitHub tells three stories: the academic ghost (85 stars, zero commits), the hardware pioneer (OSHWA-certified, then frozen), and the CC0 revolutionary (2 stars, gave away everything). This episode explores why the most important carbon capture data on GitHub is almost nobody's business.

## The Three Stories

### Story 1: The Ghost — carbon-capture-and-storage
**85★ | Last commit: March 2021 | Language: Lasso**

A BSc thesis project combining reservoir simulation, rock physics, seismic modeling, and geomechanics for CCS monitoring.

**The Commit Pattern:**
- May 2020: 5 commits in 2 days (data generation)
- Jun 2020 – Jan 2021: silence (thesis writing)
- Feb – Mar 2021: 8 commits in 5 weeks (defense prep)
- Mar 2021 – present: zero commits (4.5+ years dead)

**Why it matters:** This is the most-starred actual carbon capture simulation code we found. It's also a tombstone. 85 people starred it because they cited it, not because they used it.

**Episode quote:** *"85 stars and zero activity for four years. This repo is a citation, not a community."

### Story 2: The Pioneer — openair-cyan
**76★ | OSHWA-certified | Last commit: Feb 2024 | Physical hardware**

A DIY, small-scale, open-source direct air capture device. Not a model — a machine you can build.

**The Two-Phase Timeline:**
- Phase 1 (May – Jul 2022): 8 commits over 2.5 months — hardware development
- Phase 2 (Feb 12, 2024): 7 commits in one day — OSHWA certification + citation setup
- Phase 3 (Feb 2024 – present): silence — 2.5+ years

**Why it matters:** This is the only open-source hardware project in our entire climate tech research. It's real. You can build it. But after certification, nobody contributed.

**Episode quote:** *"They built a machine that pulls CO2 from the air. They open-sourced the plans. They got it certified as genuine open-source hardware. Then they stopped. The device works. The repo doesn't grow."

### Story 3: The Revolution — DAC Peroxovanadates & Peroxotitanates
**2★ each | CC0 License | Last commit: Sep 2025 | Computational screening data**

Two repositories containing computational screening results for carbon capture sorbent materials.

**Why they matter:** CC0 license. Not MIT. Not Apache. CC0. The authors gave up all copyright. This data is public infrastructure.

**The irony:** 2 stars for research that could help solve the climate crisis. CC0 dedication that says 'this belongs to everyone.' Almost nobody watching.

**Episode quote:** *"Two stars. CC0. Computational screening data that could lead to the next generation of carbon capture materials. The authors gave away everything. The question isn't whether it's good enough to share — it's why nobody's using it."

## The $1000/ton Challenge

The U.S. Department of Energy's goal: reduce DAC costs to $1000/ton by 2030.

**What GitHub has:**
- A directory of 2,552 climate tech projects (open-sustainable-technology)
- A bibliography of ML papers on carbon capture (Carbon_Capture_ML)
- A DIY machine you can build (OpenAir-Cyan)
- Computational screening data for new materials (DAC_peroxovanadates)

**What GitHub doesn't have:**
- A path from $600/ton to $1000/ton
- A community growing around any single carbon capture project
- A repo that's actually being built upon by multiple contributors
- Evidence that open source is accelerating deployment

**Episode angle:** "We have the map, the machine, and the materials data. What we don't have is the movement. Open source isn't failing at carbon capture. It's failing to start."

## Interview Angles / Questions for Experts

1. **To an open-source hardware maker:** "OpenAir-Cyan got OSHWA-certified in a single day. What does that certification actually mean? Does it change how people build on your work?"

2. **To a DAC researcher:** "You just published screening data for 10,000 candidate materials under CC0. Do you expect industry to use it? Or is this a public service that won't show up in anyone's citations?"

3. **To an academic who's left a repo behind:** 'Your thesis repo has 85 stars and zero commits since 2021. If you could go back, would you have kept it running? What would it have taken?'

4. **To a policy analyst:** "The DOE wants $1000/ton DAC. There are 2,552 projects in the open-sustainable-technology directory. How many of them are actually being used by the DAC industry?"
